2.	What is widely agreed upon?

Based on the systematic review by Kruse et al. (2026), the literature demonstrates clear consensus on several fundamental principles, strategies, and safeguards for healthcare cybersecurity. As a conclusion, the researchers proposed that techniques to prevent, detect, respond, and report cyberattacks largely surround proper organizational cyber resiliency.

Fundamental concepts for healthcare cybersecurity:

Patient Safety and Privacy as the Core Priority

The overarching concern of healthcare cybersecurity is safeguarding patient privacy and public safety, while organizational and financial risks remain secondary.

In-House Cyber Resiliency over Outsourcing

Establishing internal cyber resiliency, a trained workforce and a cyber-aware culture is especially important. Some healthcare administrators prefer to outsource cybersecurity operations, research indicates that relying on external sources introduces significant security vulnerabilities. Building internal resilience through continuous staff training and establishing a cyber-aware workplace culture ensures that security practices are embedded directly into daily clinical routines

Core IT Hygiene and Defense Safeguards

Core IT Hygiene and Defense Safeguards represent the fundamental, mandatory maintenance steps required to keep healthcare systems secure.

Updating and removing old tech: Applying software updates immediately while disconnecting outdated systems and legacy remote protocols (like RDP or SSH) that can no longer be safely updated.

Isolating networks: Dividing the digital network into smaller sections so a breach in one area cannot spread to the rest of the healthcare system.

Protecting and backing up data: Scrambling sensitive files with strong encryption, using Data Loss Prevention (DLP) to prevent leaks, and keeping tested copies of data offsite so systems can be restored after an incident.

Risk-Based Frameworks and Continuous Monitoring

Comprehensive defense depends on applying structured risk-management frameworks, such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, to identify mission-critical assets, alongside continuous network and device monitoring.

Augmentation with Advanced Technologies

Basic IT protections are no longer enough on their own and must be upgraded with two advanced technologies:

Artificial Intelligence (AI, AIX, Active AI): AI tools continuously inspect network traffic in real time to automatically detect and respond to cyberattacks immediately, especially across connected medical devices.

Private Blockchain: Private, permissioned blockchain networks create a secure, tamper-proof environment to protect sensitive electronic health records (EHR/EMR) and secure communications between medical devices

Direct Negative Impact on Patient Care

Cyberattacks directly threaten patient health and safety in three main ways:

Treatment Delays (55%): These included disruptions of workflow, rescheduling surgeries and nonlife threatening procedures, patient transfers, excess network traffic, and reverting to paper records.

Potential Medical Errors (27%): These included how easily infusion pumps can be altered to change rates of flow, how reverting to paper records causes manual medication dispensing, and how anti-virus software can disrupt the functioning of medical devices.

Missing Clinical Data (18%): Critical diagnostic details—such as missing care elements such as lab results or images.

Threat Modeling Quickrun: Cybersecurity in Elderly Care

1. Company

The fictional organization is a 24-hour residential care unit for older adults in Helsinki, Finland.

The unit provides continuous care and support for elderly people who require assistance with daily activities, medication administration and other healthcare needs.

The organization depends on electronic health records (EHRs) and other digital systems to provide safe and continuous care.

2. Crown Jewels

The organization’s most valuable assets include:

Data: Confidential resident information, including personal and sensitive health data. The accuracy, confidentiality and availability of this information are essential.

Business continuity: Healthcare professionals need access to residents' diagnoses, medication records and care plans to provide safe and timely care. Disruptions to digital systems affects negatively the continuity and quality of care.

Reputation and trust: Residents and their families must be able to trust the organization to provide safe care and protect sensitive information. A cybersecurity incident could damage this trust.

Money: Cybersecurity incidents may cause additional expenses related to technical recovery, increased staff workload, temporary staffing arrangements and other operational disruptions.

3. Threat Actors

The main threat actors are financially motivated external cybercriminals.

Intent: Attackers may attempt to steal confidential health information, sell stolen data or demand money through extortion.

Capability: Attackers may use phishing emails, fraudulent login pages, stolen credentials or malicious software to gain unauthorized access to information systems.

Opportunity: Healthcare professionals regularly use electronic health records and email systems. Attackers may exploit human errors, such as entering login credentials on fraudulent websites.

4. Risk Assessment

Risk 1: Phishing and Unauthorized Access to Health Information

A healthcare professional receives a phishing email and enters their login credentials on a fraudulent website. An attacker obtains the credentials and may use them to access confidential resident health information.

The consequences may include a personal data breach, additional investigation and recovery costs, increased staff workload and loss of trust.

Likelihood: 25 % per year

Estimated financial impact: €100,000

Expected annual loss: 0.25 × €100,000 = €25,000

Risk 2: Ransomware Attack and Disruption of Care

A ransomware attack prevents healthcare professionals from accessing the electronic health record system for three days.

The disruption may delay medication administration, increase the risk of medication errors and prevent residents from receiving timely and appropriate care.

The organization may also face additional costs related to IT recovery, staff overtime, temporary working arrangements and external cybersecurity support.

Likelihood: 5 % per year

Estimated financial impact: €29,000

Expected annual loss: 0.05 × €29,000 = €1,450

The financial estimates are hypothetical and have been developed for this exercise. They do not represent actual organizational costs. In particular, the financial impact of ransomware does not fully reflect the potential consequences for patient safety.

However, the potential consequences for patient safety cannot be assessed solely in financial terms. A three-day disruption to electronic health record systems may for example result in:

Delays in medication administration

Medication errors

Deterioration in residents' health conditions

In the most serious cases, patient harm or even death

5. Risk Comparison and Treatment

Based on the estimated annual financial losses, phishing and unauthorized access to health information represent the greater financial risk.

The selected risk treatment strategy is to Mitigate the risk. The organization should reduce the likelihood of successful phishing attacks through regular cybersecurity training for healthcare professionals. Training should aim to help staff recognize suspicious emails, identify fraudulent login requests and understand how to report potential security incidents. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) should also be used to provide additional protection if login credentials are stolen. Combining staff training with technical security measures can reduce the risk of unauthorized access to sensitive health information.

6. Conclusion

Cybersecurity risks in residential elderly care affect more than information systems and confidential data. They may also threaten the continuity of care and patient safety.

This simple risk assessment demonstrates the importance of identifying potential cyber threats, estimating their likelihood and impact, and selecting appropriate risk management measures.

Although phishing has the higher estimated annual financial loss in this exercise, ransomware-related disruption may have serious consequences for residents' safety. Therefore, financial estimates should not be the only consideration in healthcare cybersecurity risk management.

Take-Home Message

Cybersecurity in healthcare is not only about protecting data and information systems. It is also about protecting patients, supporting healthcare professionals, and ensuring continuity of care.

Effective cybersecurity requires a combination of technical safeguards, staff awareness, organizational preparedness, and continuous risk management.
