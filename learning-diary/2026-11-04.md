# Learning Diary – 4 November 2026

**Cyber Security: Peer Presentations, OSINT and the Defensive Perspective**

## 1. Learning from peer presentations

Today's class combined student presentations with a practical introduction to open-source intelligence (OSINT). Three presentations gave me different perspectives on cybersecurity: protecting healthcare services, protecting personal information, and understanding what can be learned from publicly available data.

### Presentation 1 – Information security in healthcare

The first presentation discussed the CIA triad (confidentiality, integrity and availability), phishing, ransomware, staff awareness, incident reporting and medical device security. I particularly liked the practical message: **Pause, Verify, Report, Protect Care**. The examples showed how everyday actions can affect the security of healthcare services. The presentation was visually engaging, although some text was rather small.

**My reflection:** In healthcare, information security is not just about protecting data. A disruption may affect continuity of care, patient safety and patients' trust. I also wondered how organizations can ensure that security instructions are understandable in multilingual workplaces. Training should support real decisions in busy clinical environments rather than remain a checklist.

### Presentation 2 – Protecting personal information

The second presentation emphasized personal data protection and practical ways individuals can improve their cybersecurity. It reminded me that personal and professional online activities can overlap.

**My reflection:** Individual choices matter, but secure behavior should not depend entirely on individuals remembering every rule. Services and organizations should make secure choices understandable and easy to use.

### Presentation 3 – Open-source intelligence (OSINT)

The third presentation introduced OSINT and included a short hands-on exercise, **Find Yourself**. I found this especially engaging because it made the concept concrete. OSINT can be used by attackers to prepare targeted messages, but defenders can also use it to identify publicly visible information and reduce exposure.

**My reflection:** A strong professional online presence is valuable for research, collaboration and networking. However, combining details from different platforms can reveal more than I intended. The goal is not to disappear from the internet, but to make deliberate choices about what I publish.

## 2. Practical exercise – Find Yourself

I explored my own digital footprint using publicly available information. The exercise was limited to my own information and passive searches; I did not attempt to access accounts or scan other people's systems.

### Searching my name

I searched my name in a private browsing window. Search results included professional material, research-related content and social media profiles. This illustrated how easily someone could assemble a profile of a person from separate public sources.

### Searching a username

I also searched a username associated with my online accounts. The results suggested links between social media profiles and my professional identity. Google displayed an AI-generated overview, which was interesting but also a reminder that automatically assembled claims are not necessarily accurate. **A search result or AI summary is a lead to verify, not proof of identity.**

### WHOIS and DNS

I tried an ICANN lookup related to my GitHub Pages site. The lookup returned the message **“No registry RDAP server was identified for this domain.”** This led me to distinguish between a personally registered domain and a site hosted under a provider's domain. I have not yet completed the DNS follow-up, so I cannot draw conclusions about the site's DNS records from this attempt.

### Historical information and other tools

The class introduced the Internet Archive and Wayback Machine as ways to explore historical versions of public websites. I have not yet verified whether my own site has archived snapshots. A voluntary Shodan exercise was also mentioned, but I have not completed it. These remain follow-up tasks rather than reported results.

## 3. From finding to defending

A key idea from the session was: **“The attacker sees what you leave open. The defender should see it first.”**

I understood the defensive value of OSINT in two related areas:

- **Threat intelligence:** understanding information and activity that may indicate potential threats.
- **Attack surface management:** identifying and reducing externally visible assets and exposures.

**My reflection:** In a healthcare organization, information about staff roles, suppliers, technology and working practices may become useful to an attacker when combined. At the same time, publicly available information is not automatically a vulnerability. The important questions are whether the information is accurate, how it could be misused, and which safeguards are proportionate. This connects OSINT with organizational resilience and patient safety.

## 4. Questions and new learning goals

### Multi-factor authentication

I want to compare different MFA methods, particularly passkeys and FIDO2 security keys, authenticator apps and SMS codes. My main question is how to balance resistance to phishing with usability and reliable account recovery.

### Checking suspicious emails and files

I want to learn how tools such as VirusTotal can support investigation of suspicious links and attachments. A crucial limitation is confidentiality: patient data, work documents or other sensitive files should not be uploaded to public analysis services.

### VPN reliability

I have experienced connectivity problems when using a VPN. This made me consider an important practical question: how can security controls remain effective if they interfere with normal access? I would like to investigate the technical cause rather than assume that disabling protection is the solution.

### Building presentations with GitHub Pages

One student used an interactive HTML-based presentation, which inspired me to explore a reusable presentation template of my own. I would like to learn how **HTML** structures slides, **CSS** controls their appearance, and **JavaScript** enables navigation. This would build naturally on the GitHub and Markdown skills I have developed during the course.

### Cybersecurity community

The teacher recommended exploring **HelSec**, a Finnish cybersecurity community. I would like to learn about its events and opportunities to exchange ideas with people from different professional backgrounds. I could contribute a healthcare and research perspective while developing my technical understanding.

## 5. Overall reflection

The most valuable aspect of today's class was the combination of peer learning, a practical exercise and critical reflection. The OSINT exercise made me more aware of how publicly available information can be connected across platforms. It also helped me see the difference between simply finding information and assessing its accuracy and security relevance.

My professional background shapes how I interpret these issues. Cybersecurity in healthcare is a sociotechnical challenge: technology, leadership, staff competence, communication and organizational practices all influence whether essential services remain safe and available.

**My key takeaway:** Cybersecurity awareness includes knowing what is visible about us and our organizations, verifying what we find, and using that knowledge to reduce risks without unnecessarily limiting useful openness.
