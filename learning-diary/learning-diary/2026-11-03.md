# Learning Diary – 3 November 2026

## H2 Scene: Connecting Cybersecurity Research with Real-World Incidents

Today, I completed and submitted the H2 Scene assignment. The assignment included exploring cybersecurity podcasts and blogs, connecting current cybersecurity incidents with scientific research, and practising technical skills through Debian Linux and RSS feeds.

### Connecting Research and Practice

The most valuable part of this assignment was connecting the Stryker cyberattack reported by Krebs (2026) with the systematic review by Akeredolu et al. (2027).

The Stryker incident helped me understand how a cyberattack against an external technology provider can affect healthcare organisations, even when hospitals are not the direct targets. Healthcare depends on medical devices, digital services and reliable supply chains. Disruptions affecting these dependencies can threaten continuity of care.

Akeredolu et al. (2027) provided a broader theoretical perspective. Their research highlighted the challenges of integrating cyber threat intelligence into healthcare risk assessment, particularly the gaps between technical cybersecurity solutions and their practical implementation.

I found the concept of **clinician-centred explainability** especially important. Cybersecurity information must be understandable and relevant to healthcare professionals. Technical alerts alone are insufficient if nurses and doctors cannot recognise their potential consequences for clinical work and patient safety.

This strengthened my understanding that cybersecurity risk management requires collaboration between IT specialists, healthcare professionals, organisational leadership and external technology providers.

### Zero Trust and Least Privilege

Listening to the Darknet Diaries episode about ThreatLocker helped me better understand Zero Trust and the principle of least privilege.

One important insight was that Zero Trust does not mean preventing people from accessing information. Instead, access should be verified and limited according to legitimate work-related needs.

In healthcare, this creates an interesting challenge: professionals must have timely access to essential patient information while organisations must prevent unauthorised access and the spread of cyberattacks.

I also recognised that cybersecurity solutions need to support clinical workflows rather than unnecessarily complicate them.

### Cyber Threat Intelligence and Organisational Resilience

The Stryker incident raised new questions about cybersecurity-related information sharing.

How can hospitals identify emerging threats affecting their external suppliers? How should cybersecurity information be communicated to healthcare professionals? And how can organisations prepare for disruptions before they affect patient care?

I increasingly see cybersecurity resilience as an organisational capability rather than merely a technical one.

From my perspective as a healthcare educator and researcher, staff education should extend beyond online courses. Practical exercises, realistic scenarios and open discussion of cybersecurity incidents could help healthcare professionals understand how cyber threats affect their daily work.
